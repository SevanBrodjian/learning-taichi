Sand is now canonical physics and it is cheap — snow, not sand, still sets the demo's frame budget — but chasing the angle of repose exposed that the settled slope of every plastic material here decays with substep count, and snow's decays almost entirely.
What happened. Sand entered sim/physics/ as a Drucker-Prager granular material with its own golden signature, all pre-existing signatures still green, and the frozen materials provably untouched. It runs at dt = 1e-4, i.e. 167 substeps/frame at 60 fps, the same as elastic, so snow remains the binding constraint at 333 and adding sand to a scene that already contains snow costs exactly nothing. (Sand alone would raise a water-only scene from 139 to 167.) Four materials now share one grid through a per-particle material id, and a single material pushed through that path matches the canonical one to below its own run-to-run noise. What did not work. The angle of repose is not a converged quantity. Held for 4 s and then refined fourfold in the timestep, sand's settled slope goes from 24° to 19°, and canonical snow's collapses from 56° to 19°. Both track the number of substeps taken rather than the physical time elapsed, so refining the timestep makes them worse, not better.

01One grid means one timestep

An explicit solver's frame cost is substeps x cost-per-substep, and substeps = (1/60)/dt. A shared grid carries a single velocity field advanced once per substep, so every material in it runs at the same dt — the smallest one present. Pick a material mix and watch what it bills.

02Is it sand? The over-steep heap

The same 60° triangular pile, released from rest, under each of the four materials. A drop test cannot tell these apart — everything looks like a splat at impact. This can.

Each panel reports its own free-surface slope as it evolves. Water runs flat. Elastic and snow keep essentially the whole seeded slope. Sand slumps partway and stops.

Elastic and snow keeping the slope is the informative part. Snow's yield criterion is a fixed box on elastic stretch, so it does not weaken as confining pressure drops — snow has cohesion and can stand a wall. Sand's admissible set is a cone whose width is proportional to pressure, so near a free surface it has no strength at all. The slope where its own weight supplies just enough confinement is the angle of repose, and nothing in the model prescribes it.

The friction angle is not the angle of repose

Measured angle against the model's friction-angle parameter, over 4 seeds x 3 pile sizes. Tight and size-independent up to about 50°; past that the error bars explode and the angle starts tracking how many grid cells the pile spans, which is a discretisation artifact rather than a material property. Canonical sand is frozen at the top of the reproducible band.

03The trap: “stable” is not the criterion

Every canonical material tolerates a far larger timestep than it uses without ever going non-finite. That fact is useless, and acting on it is how a simulation quietly becomes a different physical system. Flip between the two readings of the same sweep.

04Why: the plastic materials creep per substep, not per second

Refining the timestep does not converge the settled pile, it changes it, and always in the same direction. Which axis the curves collapse on says whether that is physics or bookkeeping. Flip the horizontal axis.

The heap test again, all four materials simultaneously in one simulation rather than four.
Contact between different materials is whatever a shared node velocity produces: two materials meeting at a node exchange momentum as if the node held one blended material. That is coexistence, not a calibrated multi-phase contact model, and nothing on this page is a claim about what happens at a sand-water interface.

06The refactor changed nothing

A single material pushed through the new branching path has to land where the canonical compile-time path lands. Equality is the wrong bar: GPU atomic scatter order varies run to run, and the two paths compile different kernels so they can order the same arithmetic differently at the last bit, which a chaotic rollout then amplifies. The bar is therefore a bracket of disagreements that provably carry no information — run-to-run nondeterminism at the bottom, and re-running canonical with the initial positions nudged by one float32 rounding unit at the top.

All figures are traj_rmse: mean per-particle distance over the whole rollout, in domain lengths, over repeats of each path. The refactor's disagreement is at or below the one-ulp nudge for every material, i.e. smaller than the effect of perturbing the initial condition by a rounding error.
The shape is the evidence, not the endpoint. A real bias would appear immediately and grow linearly. These start at rounding scale and grow exponentially into the same plateau as the grey band, which is chaos amplifying a last-bit difference and nothing else.

The frozen materials also had to be untouched. A single before/after comparison would prove nothing here, because two runs of the same code already differ by up to 1e-2 on the chaotic fluid column. So the test is distributional: repeats of each configuration under the pre-promotion physics (restored from git) and under the new one, then every pairwise distance within a version against every distance across versions. Across-version distances come out at the within-version mean at worst, over fluid, elastic and snow on two scenes — indistinguishable. Physics version went → .

Within-code distances are the simulator disagreeing with itself; across-code are before-the-promotion against after. If the promotion had moved a frozen material, the right-hand column would sit outside the left-hand range.
Scope. 2D, one grid resolution (128²), one floor friction, particle counts in the low thousands, forward simulation only. The angle of repose is measured on one scene family.
